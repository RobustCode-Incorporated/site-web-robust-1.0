# Product Quality Standard

Before any product moves from `status: draft` to `status: published`, it must have a written answer — not just an implied one — to each of the following. If any answer is missing or vague, the product is not ready.

## Problem
What painful or expensive problem does this solve? (Not "helps with X" — specifically what goes wrong today without it, and what that costs someone in time, money, or risk.)

## Audience
Who specifically needs it? Named roles, not "entrepreneurs" or "business owners" in the abstract.

## Outcome
What can the buyer concretely accomplish with it that they couldn't before? Stated as a capability, not a promise of results ("you can produce a documented project scope in an afternoon," not "your projects will succeed").

## Mechanism
Why does the system work? What's the actual reasoning connecting the method to the outcome? A buyer should be able to explain this back after reading the product page.

## Implementation
How does the buyer actually use it, step by step? If this can't be described in a few sentences, the "How it works" section on the product page needs more work, not less.

## Deliverables
Exactly what files/resources are included — matching `includes[]` in the frontmatter precisely. Never list something in marketing copy that isn't actually in the package.

## Limitations
What does it NOT do? Every product needs this section, even informally in the FAQ. This is what separates an honest product page from a sales page.

## Absolute rules (no exceptions)

- Never promise a guaranteed business outcome.
- Never fabricate a testimonial, review, user count, or "as seen in" claim.
- Never use manufactured scarcity (fake countdown, fake limited stock) for a digital product that has no actual supply constraint.
- Never copy another creator's framework, templates, or copyrighted material.
- Never publish financial, legal, or regulatory guidance inside a product without it being reviewed by someone qualified to make that claim — and say so plainly if it hasn't been.
